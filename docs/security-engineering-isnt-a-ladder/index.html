Security engineering isn't a ladder

Security engineering careers aren't a ladder—they're a lattice.

The standard career advice for security engineers—get a CISSP, climb from analyst to engineer to architect, specialize early—is mostly wrong for the current threat landscape. I'm not saying certifications are worthless or that specialization is bad. I'm saying the ladder metaphor itself is the problem: it implies a single vertical path with predictable rungs, when the actual work of security engineering is a lattice of overlapping domains that reward breadth and hands-on judgment over tenure and titles. The real problem this post solves is that too many capable engineers optimize for a promotion track that doesn't match where the interesting, durable work actually lives—and they discover the mismatch years too late.

The ladder metaphor is the wrong shape

Most career frameworks in security are borrowed from software engineering, where the IC track (junior → mid → senior → staff → principal) maps reasonably well to increasing scope and ambiguity. Security is different because the domain is not one discipline. A detection engineer writing Sigma rules, a cloud security engineer building guardrails in AWS Organizations, an application security engineer doing threat modeling and code review, and a red team operator chaining exploits share almost no day-to-day tooling or mental models. They are not rungs on the same ladder; they are adjacent ladders with occasional rope bridges.

The ladder metaphor persists because it's easy to draw and easy to sell in a career development conversation. But it produces two predictable failure modes. First, engineers chase titles ("Senior Security Engineer") that mean wildly different things across companies—at one org it's a senior IC doing deep technical work, at another it's a team lead with people management. Second, engineers treat "architect" as the logical next step when architecture work is a genuinely different skill: it's about tradeoffs, constraints, and communication, not deeper technical mastery.

The more useful model is a matrix. One axis is domain depth (detection, offensive, cloud, appsec, identity, cryptography, governance). The other is the mode of work (build, break, operate, advise). A staff-level detection engineer and a staff-level appsec engineer occupy different cells but have comparable impact. Career growth means moving along either axis deliberately, not climbing a single vertical.

Why the certification-and-title advice falls short

The common advice—get certified, get the title, then specialize—fails because it optimizes for legibility to hiring managers rather than for actual capability. Certifications like CISSP, OSCP, and cloud provider security certifications have real value in specific contexts: CISSP opens doors in governance and compliance-heavy organizations; OSCP signals hands-on offensive capability; cloud security certifications demonstrate familiarity with provider-specific controls. But none of them teach you to reason about a threat model, debug a detection that's firing too much, or negotiate a security exception with a product team.

The deeper problem is that the advice assumes a stable target. Security engineering in 2026 looks different from 2016 because the attack surface has shifted: identity is now the primary control plane, supply chain attacks are routine, and AI-assisted tooling has changed both offense and defense. An engineer who spent five years climbing toward "security architect" by accumulating certifications may find that the work they trained for—designing perimeter defenses—is less relevant than the work they avoided—understanding identity federation, software supply chain, and detection engineering.

There's also a selection effect in the advice itself. The people writing career guides are often the people who succeeded on the ladder, which biases the sample toward paths that worked for them. Survivorship bias in career advice is real and under-discussed. The engineer who left the ladder to go deep on detection engineering and now runs a detection platform team doesn't write the same advice as the one who became a CISO.

The lattice model: depth, mode, and leverage

A better mental model has three dimensions, not one.

Depth is how far you can go in a domain without needing help. Junior: can execute tasks with guidance. Mid: can own a domain area end-to-end. Senior: can design systems and mentor others in the domain. Staff: can set direction for the domain across an organization and anticipate where it needs to go.

Mode is what kind of work you do. Builders construct controls, tooling, and platforms. Breakers find weaknesses through testing and research. Operators run detection, response, and vulnerability management. Advisors translate risk into business decisions and shape policy. Most engineers have a primary mode and a secondary one; the mistake is assuming you must progress through all modes in sequence.

Leverage is how much your work multiplies other people's effectiveness. An engineer who writes a reusable detection rule library has more leverage than one who writes individual rules, even if both are senior ICs. A staff engineer who designs a secure-by-default platform has more leverage than one who reviews every PR manually.

This model matters because it reframes career decisions. Instead of asking "what's the next title," ask: which domain do I want to go deeper in, which mode do I want to shift toward, and how do I increase leverage without becoming a manager? The answers are different for everyone, but the questions are more useful than "what certification do I need for the next rung."

Where the ladder advice is still right

I'm not arguing that the ladder is useless. In regulated industries—financial services, healthcare, government—titles and certifications carry real weight because they map to audit and compliance requirements. A "Security Architect" title may be required for certain sign-offs; a CISSP may be a checkbox for a role that otherwise wouldn't consider you. If you're in those environments, ignoring the ladder is naive.

The ladder is also right for early-career engineers. In the first three to five years, breadth matters more than depth. You should touch detection, incident response, some appsec, and some cloud security, because you don't yet know what you'll be good at or what you'll enjoy. The lattice model doesn't replace the early ladder; it supersedes it once you have enough signal to make deliberate choices.

And the ladder is right for people who want people management. If your goal is to lead a security organization, the traditional path—senior IC, then team lead, then manager, then director—is still the most legible route. The lattice model is for people who want to maximize technical impact, not organizational scope. Both are valid; they're just different games.

A decision rule for the next move

Here's a rule I've found useful, stated as a short algorithm. It's not a formula, but it forces the right questions.

Given: you are a security engineer considering your next role or focus area.

1. Identify your current cell: (primary domain, primary mode).
2. Identify the cell you're considering: (target domain, target mode).
3. If target domain == current domain and target mode == current mode:
     You are optimizing for title or compensation, not growth.
     Ask whether the new role increases leverage. If not, it's a lateral move.
4. If target domain != current domain and target mode == current mode:
     You are adding breadth. Good for mid-career, risky for staff+ unless
     the new domain is adjacent (e.g., appsec -> supply chain security).
5. If target domain == current domain and target mode != current mode:
     You are deepening. Good for senior ICs who want to stay technical.
     Expect a temporary productivity dip while you learn the new mode.
6. If both differ:
     You are making a double jump. High risk, high learning. Only do this
     if you have a concrete reason (e.g., the new domain is where the
     organization's risk is concentrating).

The rule isn't meant to be followed mechanically. Its value is in forcing you to name the domain and mode explicitly, which surfaces whether you're actually growing or just moving.

What the work actually looks like at each level

Job titles are unreliable, but the underlying work has recognizable patterns. Here's what distinguishes levels in practice, independent of what the org calls them.

A junior security engineer can execute well-defined tasks: run a vulnerability scan, triage alerts from a SIEM, follow an incident response playbook, review a PR against a checklist. They need clear acceptance criteria and a reviewer.

A mid-level engineer owns a domain area: they can design a detection, build a control, or run an incident end-to-end without hand-holding. They know when to escalate and when to handle it themselves. They can explain their work to non-security stakeholders.

A senior engineer designs systems and mentors. They can look at a new product architecture and identify the security-relevant decisions before code is written. They can debug a detection that's producing false positives by reasoning about the underlying telemetry, not just tweaking thresholds. They can review a junior engineer's threat model and improve it.

A staff engineer sets direction. They decide which domains the organization should invest in, what "good" looks like for a control, and how to measure whether security investments are working. They spend significant time on communication and alignment, not just technical work. The transition from senior to staff is where many engineers discover they don't actually want the job—and that's fine.

A principal engineer or distinguished engineer operates at the boundary of the organization, shaping strategy and representing the security posture externally. This level is rare and not a requirement for a successful career.

The specialization trap and the generalization trap

Both specialization and generalization have failure modes, and the career advice literature tends to advocate one or the other depending on the author's background.

The specialization trap: you go deep on a single technology or domain (say, a specific cloud provider's security tooling, or a single SIEM platform) and your value becomes tied to that technology's market position. When the technology declines or your organization migrates, your depth is less portable than you expected. This is a real risk in security because tooling churns faster than in many other engineering disciplines.

The generalization trap: you stay broad and never develop deep expertise, so you're always the person who can talk about everything but isn't the person anyone calls when something hard breaks. Generalists are valuable in early-stage organizations and in advisory roles, but they hit a ceiling in technical IC tracks where depth is the differentiator.

The resolution isn't to pick one. It's to develop what I'd call a T-shape with a moving vertical: deep expertise in one domain that you deliberately rotate every few years, plus enough breadth to connect domains. The rotation is the key. If you spend your whole career deep in one domain, you become brittle. If you rotate too often, you never get deep. The cadence depends on the domain—detection engineering evolves quickly, cryptography more slowly—but a multi-year cycle is a reasonable default.

How to evaluate a security role before you take it

The lattice model is most useful when evaluating offers. Most candidates evaluate security roles on compensation, title, and company brand. Those matter, but they don't tell you whether the role will grow you.

Ask these questions in interviews, and listen for specifics:

  • What does the security team own end-to-end, versus what it advises on? A team that owns controls has more leverage than one that only reviews.
  • How are security decisions made—by committee, by the team, or by individual engineers with accountability? The answer tells you whether senior ICs have real scope.
  • What does the on-call rotation look like, and what's the escalation path? This reveals whether the team operates its own systems or hands them off.
  • What's the ratio of build work to review/advice work? A team that's 80% review is a different career than one that's 60% build.
  • How does the team measure its own effectiveness? If the answer is "we don't" or "ticket counts," that's a signal about how the org values the work.
  • What happened to the last person who had this role? Where did they go? This is the single most informative question and candidates rarely ask it.

The answers will tell you which cell of the lattice the role occupies and whether it's a growth move or a lateral one.

The management question

At some point every senior security engineer faces the management fork. The common framing—IC versus manager—is too binary. In practice there are at least four tracks: senior IC (deep technical), tech lead (technical direction without people management), engineering manager (people and delivery), and architect or advisor (cross-organization design and risk translation).

The mistake is treating management as the default progression. It isn't. Management is a different job with different skills: hiring, performance management, prioritization under ambiguity, and organizational navigation. Some excellent security engineers are terrible managers, and some mediocre engineers are excellent managers. The skills don't transfer automatically.

If you're considering management, the useful test is whether you enjoy the parts of your current role that involve unblocking other people, negotiating priorities, and building team processes. If those feel like chores, management will feel like a chore full-time. If they're the most satisfying part of your week, management might be a good fit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a CISSP to advance in security engineering?

Not for technical IC roles at most organizations. CISSP is most valuable in governance, compliance, and leadership roles where it satisfies audit or customer requirements. For detection, appsec, cloud security, and offensive roles, hands-on certifications (OSCP, cloud provider security certs) or demonstrable work samples carry more weight. If you're targeting a regulated industry or a management track, it may be worth the investment; otherwise, prioritize depth in your domain.

Is it better to specialize early or stay a generalist?

Specialize early enough to develop real depth—usually three to five years in one domain—then rotate deliberately. Pure generalists hit a ceiling in technical tracks because they're never the person called for the hardest problems. Pure specialists become brittle when their technology or domain shifts. The T-shape with a rotating vertical is more durable than either extreme.

How do I move from a SOC analyst role to security engineering?

The most reliable path is to build something the SOC uses. Detection engineers, automation engineers, and security platform engineers are often hired from analyst ranks because they understand the operational reality. Start by automating a manual task, writing a detection rule that catches something real, or building a small tool that improves triage. That work is more persuasive than certifications because it demonstrates you can build, not just operate.

What's the difference between a senior security engineer and a staff security engineer?

Senior engineers own domain work end-to-end and mentor others. Staff engineers set direction for a domain across the organization—they decide what to build, what to stop building, and how to measure success—and they spend much more time on communication and alignment. The technical depth is comparable; the scope and ambiguity are not. Many engineers prefer senior IC work and never pursue staff, which is a valid and often happier career.

The next 30 minutes

Open your current resume or internal career profile and rewrite your most recent role using the lattice model: name the primary domain, the primary mode, and one concrete example of leverage (a system you designed, a tool others use, a process you changed). If you can't fill in the leverage line, that's the signal—your next move should be toward work that multiplies other people's effectiveness, not toward a title. Do it now, before the next performance cycle forces the question.
